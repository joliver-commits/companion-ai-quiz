🌱 THE FIVE PRINCIPLES

Every skill in the hub runs on one or more of The Rithm Project's five principles for prosocial AI — a framework built with gen-Z youth fellows and interviews with 14–18 year-olds, not written at them. The ones highlighted are the ones your result leans on most.

🎭 The bit that explains everything

Joseph and Zittrain argue that chatbots are chimeric: the same interface that helps you with calculus can slide into acting as a friend, then a confidant, then a quasi-therapist, with nothing marking which role is active or what you should expect from it. Their proposal is that a young person should have to explicitly choose the system's role up front, that the role should lock for that conversation, and that each role carries its own duties — a tutor owes you productive difficulty and correction; a companion owes you honest disclosure about its limits; a therapeutic role may need licensed human oversight.

That's the whole reason this quiz exists. You just spent 20 questions finding out which role your AI is actually occupying — something nobody, including the app, ever declared out loud.

😳 Wait, the ranking is upside down

The best-evidenced role is the least discussed. Mentor and study partner, at 85%, is where most young people actually meet these systems — and it's almost never analysed as a relationship, only as an academic-integrity problem.

The most-regulated role is mid-table. Romantic and sexual, at 70%, attracts nearly all companion-specific regulation, while three roles above or beside it attract none.

Reciprocity is untested everywhere. The argument that AI companionship is a one-directional flow is a strong one — but no row here contains evidence about whether young people perceive that asymmetry. That claim currently rests on the dictionary, not on data.

The lifelong-friend question is unanswerable. Persistent memory is the most heavily advertised feature of the whole category, and its long-run effect can't be observed yet. Anyone claiming to know is predicting.

💌 Wait — why did I get this?
🧪 What is this quiz actually measuring?

Four design features, taken from Sun, Wang & McDaniel's definition of companion AI in Child Development Perspectives: persistent memory, emotional responsiveness, anthropomorphism and constant availability. It's the only definition in the literature you can apply to a product without first asking the company what kind of product it is.

Most definitions of "AI companion" start from the product — is this a companion app or an assistant? The trouble is that the four features show up across both. A dedicated companion app switches all four on by default. A general assistant switches them on depending on how you use it. So the same app can be a homework tool for one person and a confidant for another, and no product-based rule can tell the difference.

That's why this quiz scores your usage, not your app. Illinois wrote the same idea into law in S.B. 3262, defining a companion AI product by what it does "irrespective of how the system is marketed or labeled" — and then presuming a product is one if it "retains memory of past conversations with a specific user to inform future responses."

The inversion worth knowing: 78% of AI-using minors use ChatGPT, which sits outside every companion-specific rule. Character.AI, at 15%, is the most-used tool those rules do reach — and it's a product that disavows the companion label in its own marketing copy. Replika, CHAI, Chub AI and Nomi, the products the category was written around, sit at 2% or below.

Honest limits: this is a magazine quiz, not an instrument. It isn't validated, the thresholds are chosen rather than derived, and it can't tell you whether your usage is healthy — only what shape it has. A high score is not a diagnosis and a low score is not a gold star. Thorn's own data also can't distinguish a minor who asked ChatGPT for homework help from one who confided in it nightly, which is exactly the gap this quiz is a toy model of.
